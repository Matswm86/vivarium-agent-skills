## Every cure protocol is really one rule: full cure before water and animals

The hobby blames mould inhibitor. A detailed technical treatment disagrees and names the real cause:

> "'I just resealed my tank and mistakenly used mold resistant silicone. I killed all my fish.' **The mold resistance did not kill the fish. The silicone hadn't completely cured, and the fish died from chemicals in the uncured silicone.**"

Same source: "The fungicide is bonded into the structure of the silicone, so it is NOT soluble into the aquarium water"; the quantity "is so incredibly tiny it would not even hurt sensitive creatures like shrimp."

**The stronger reason to buy aquarium-branded anyway is D4, not fungicide.** Aquarium-safe silicones carry **<=0.2% D4 (octamethylcyclotetrasiloxane)** by weight versus **1-5% in general-purpose** — up to 25x more. D4 bioaccumulates; for <=1 g rainbow trout the acute LC50 is **0.01 mg/L**. *(Caveat: a hobby-technical site quoting a commenter; the D4 percentages are not independently verified.)*

**Two corrections to common lore:**
- **"Brand X Silicone 1 has no mildewcide" is FALSE for current products.** Major general-purpose silicone lines now advertise 7-year and 10-year mould-free product protection across both cure chemistries. The old "grade 1 is safe, grade 2 is the additive one" rule no longer matches what is on sale.
- **The manufacturers themselves say do not submerge them:** "If your project involves full submersion (pools, whirlpools, boats, or aquariums), use a specialty underwater or marine-grade sealant." That warning label is not liability boilerplate.

**"100% silicone" only means no other polymer is in the formulation.** What to actually avoid is a **copolymer**, which blends in a cheaper plastic whose "acrylic portion doesn't bond as well to glass and can be attacked by fungi."

**Citable pre-livestock protocols** (in place of the popular vinegar-bath test, which **UNVERIFIED** — no citable source describes it):

1. **Extended damp cure, then cycle.** Leave a damp rag in the enclosure under plastic sheet for **at least two weeks** (the humidity drives the moisture cure — manufacturers independently recommend "place a container filled with warm water in the aquarium or terrarium"), then **six weeks of filter cycling including two 95% water changes** before adding fish.
2. **Manufacturer minimum:** an aquarium acetoxy at ~2 mm/24 h needs **at least 4 days before filling**. "Never fill the aquarium until full cure."
3. **The tack test** (epoxy): confirm no tacky areas remain before filling. Pond-rated epoxy is water-and-fish-ready at 24 h, +12 h in cool conditions.
4. **The smell endpoint** (acetoxy): rinsing with pure water "removes the last remains" of acetic acid. Absence of vinegar smell is the manufacturer-implied endpoint, though no formal test is described.
5. **Do not bury uncured sealant:** "When using different reactive joint sealants, the first joint sealant must be completely hardened before the next one is applied."

**Cured PU foam is chemically finished.** The isocyanate hazard is **pre-cure only** — "The can contains a quick reacting hazardous chemical, isocyanate, to form the non-hazardous plastic."

**CORRECTION — "some PU foams contain fungicide additives" is UNVERIFIED.** No manufacturer page or TDS retrieved declares a fungicide, mildewcide, or insecticide in any PU foam; the one pest-marketed foam is explicitly "Pesticide-free… Won't harm bees," blocking pests mechanically. **The documented biocide issue is in silicones, not foams.** Do not repeat the biocide-in-foam claim as sourced.
