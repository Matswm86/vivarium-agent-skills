## Prep protocols conflict — here is both sides

**The standard aquascaping protocol:** strip bark and brush clean, submerge weighted **~1 week**, boil **~1 h repeated 3-4 times**, submerge another **~1 week**, repeating until the soak water runs clear. Retail guidance is simpler: boil 1-2 h generically; cholla only 20 min boil or 24-48 h soak.

**The contrarian view, and it is specific:** "Boiling or baking wood accomplishes nothing… Boiling wood will break down the wood structure and cause the wood to soften. **Boiled wood CAN in some cases deteriorate much quicker in an aquarium than unboiled wood.**" The same source calls "boil the wood to make it sink" a myth — "the only good solution is to just weight it down with rocks" — and warns that baking combustible wood in an oven is a fire risk.

**Low-risk synthesis: long weighted soak plus mechanical anchoring, with boiling reserved for sterilising small pieces.**

**Sterilisation numbers (reptile/bioactive side):** wood baked at **~93 C for 1 h**, boiled **20 min**, or **frozen >=2 weeks**. Large pieces: scrub with hot water, sun-dry on a tarp several days, turning daily. Leaves: **~60 C for ~20 min**, boiled 10 min, or frozen >=2 weeks; one supplier uses a **-26 C deep freeze for 48 h**. Rocks: boil 30 min.

**Wood selection:**

| Wood | Behaviour in permanent wet | Prep |
|---|---|---|
| **Mopani** | Density 1075 kg/m3, rot resistance "very durable," termite/beetle resistant, sinks immediately. Heavy, so hard to mount safely to glass. Tannin release is heavy and **prolonged**, and will shift pH | Boil 1-2 h and/or soak days; expect tint for weeks |
| **Spiderwood / azalea root** | **Floats.** Releases few humic acids so it barely moves pH. Commonly throws a harmless white biofilm early | Soak ~1 week weighted, or boil 1-2 h; weight with stone |

## The white bloom on new wood is harmless

Two overlapping sourced explanations:
- **Residual-sugar bacterial film:** "These bacteria utilize residual sugar still present in the root. After depletion, the bacterial film will disappear by itself. It poses no threat."
- **Water mould (oomycete), the more precise account:** not a fungus but one of ~250 species in the order **Saprolegniales**, more closely related to diatoms than to mushrooms. "Water molds require a source of carbohydrates or proteins to grow. They are common on wood in the aquarium feeding off the decomposing cellulose… They are unsightly but harmless." Colour varies with the wood — white, grey, red on high-tannin wood, purple on some bogwood.

**"Water mold on wood will NOT transfer to a fish and infect the fish."** Shrimp and snails actively eat it. **UNVERIFIED: no source addresses risk to *Geosesarma* or other crabs specifically.**

**Duration:** aquarium side "typically only lasts four or five weeks"; vivarium side "most 'new vivarium' mold issues disappear within a couple weeks, as the microfauna devours it."

**It is a feature, not a fault:** "This initial mold cycle acting as a natural food source for beneficial microfauna is one of the reasons builders let vivariums cycle for at least a month before adding inhabitants." Larger enclosures "should always use both springtails + isopods." **Do not dose antibiotics or antibacterials** — they "will damage and pollute the biosystem."

**Escalate when:** the mould persists past a few months, the smell turns bad, or the wood begins to disintegrate — then "it is too soft and/or weathered to be in the water and will have to come out." Mould **on the substrate itself** "greatly increases the risk of an anaerobic condition."

**Side effect worth knowing:** a lot of fresh wood in a low-tech planted water section causes "all the available nitrogen being absorbed by the water molds and fungi feeding on the carbohydrates in the wood" — expect a nitrogen dip in the first weeks.

The closest sourced statement is that mould "will be more apparent in highly humid vivariums… below 80% may have little to no visible growth," which implies the effect without stating the remedy. Also: Saprolegniales are described in the mycology literature as obligate-aquatic ("entirely dependent on water"), so the water-mould identification holds for submerged or waterlogged wood; a white bloom on merely humid land-side wood or moss is more likely a true fungus, and no source in this file covers that case.
