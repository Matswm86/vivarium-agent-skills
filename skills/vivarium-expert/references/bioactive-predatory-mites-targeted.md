## The one crew member with field numbers is missing from most crews

Springtails and isopods do not control fungus gnats. The agent with actual trial data is the predatory mite *Stratiolaelaps scimitus* (ex *Hypoaspis miles*) — and it is a double-edged addition.

**Wrong:**

```
Fungus gnats → hope the springtails handle it     # no sourced gnat predation by springtails
Gnats entrenched → add mites as a rescue          # documented as unreliable once established
```

**Right:**

```
Add S. scimitus EARLY, at first gnat sighting, as a targeted temporary treatment.
It hunts the top ~13 mm of moist soil; it cannot tolerate standing water
(paludarium wet zones are outside its reach).
```

**Why:** Penn State extension data: one mite kills more than 11 fly larvae in 3 days; treated mushroom rooms showed 40% lower phorid emergence; egg-to-adult takes 10-34 days; females lay ~3 eggs per 2 days. The same literature lists its prey as fly larvae plus springtails — a resident mite population suppresses the clean-up crew you seeded. The other sourced gnat biocontrol is *Steinernema* nematodes.

**Verify:** yellow sticky card above the substrate; count adult gnats per week before and 3 weeks after introduction.

**Sources:**
- https://extension.psu.edu/all-about-stratiolaelaps-scimitus-hypoaspis-miles-predatory-mites — kill rate, field trial, development times
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratiolaelaps_scimitus — springtail predation, top-soil hunting depth, standing-water intolerance

**Unverified:** the population-level outcome of mites coexisting with an established springtail colony (suppression vs equilibrium) has no study; "targeted and temporary" is precautionary reasoning, not a cited protocol.
