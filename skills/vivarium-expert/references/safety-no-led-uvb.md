## LED UVB is commercially real but not evidence-validated

Baines et al. 2023, "UVB-emitting LEDs for reptile lighting: Identifying the risks of nonsolar UV spectra," spectrally analysed **18 commercial UVB-LED lamps** and found:

- spectra bore "little similarity" to natural sunlight;
- some emitted **non-terrestrial UVB below 290-295 nm and UVC below 280 nm** — wavelengths that do not reach the Earth's surface at all;
- **one documented case of acute photokeratoconjunctivitis** attributed to such a lamp;
- all tested lamps **lacked output in the 315-335 nm band** that in natural sunlight helps self-limit cutaneous vitamin D3 overproduction.

The authors call for long-term animal studies with serum 25(OH)D3 monitoring before LED-UVB can be treated as equivalent in safety to fluorescent or mercury vapour.

**Verdict: use T5 HO fluorescent UVB.**

**Fair caveat, stated:** this is one 2023 spectral survey of 18 lamps, and the paper calls for more study rather than declaring LED-UVB unsafe outright. Products do exist (e.g. Zoo Med ReptiSun UVB/LED Gen 2, 18 W, claimed 295-320 nm UVB, working distance 20-100 cm) but publish **no UVI figures**.

**The overexposure injury to know:** photokeratoconjunctivitis (PKC) — corneal and conjunctival damage, described as extremely painful, onset within roughly 12 hours of exposure.

**Universal mitigation across every source: provide a UV and shade gradient, never a uniformly UV-flooded enclosure.** The animal self-regulates by moving. In a tall densely planted paludarium this happens naturally; in a short open terrarium it must be designed in deliberately with cork, hides, and planted cover.
