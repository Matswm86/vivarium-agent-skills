## Density is not the problem — equal visual weight is

Three named hardscape grammars transfer directly from aquascaping:

- **Nature Aquarium** (Amano, 1990s): "asymmetrical arrangement of masses of relatively few species," hardscape positioned off-centre, rooted in Japanese garden design — Wabi-sabi and Zen rock arrangement.
- **Iwagumi:** a stone grammar where "each stone has a name and a specific role" — **Oyaishi** (primary focal stone, off-centre, never dead-centre), **Soeishi** (secondary stones grouped to reinforce it), **Fukuseki** (subordinate fill).
- **Dutch style:** dense planting (>80% substrate coverage), many species at different heights and textures, essentially no hardscape focal point. Useful as the contrast case.

**Translated into 3D:** one dominant vertical mass (main branch/root system plus waterfall column), placed off-centre on the width axis, with secondary masses grouped near it and smaller tertiary pieces filling the rest — **never a second mass of equal visual weight.**

**Placement arithmetic:** a 150 cm width divided by the golden ratio puts an off-centre focal line at roughly **93 cm or 57 cm** from either edge (150 / 1.618 = 92.7, my calc). Rule of thirds gives 50 cm and 100 cm. Either is legitimate; **centring is the one placement both systems agree to avoid.**

**The four real clutter drivers:**
1. **No dominance hierarchy.** Every messy large paludarium has multiple masses competing at the same visual weight with nothing telling the eye where to land first. The Oyaishi/Soeishi/Fukuseki naming exists to enforce one dominant, several supporting, many minor.
2. **No negative space.** Filling all ~1.68 m3 with visual incident removes the rest areas the eye needs between focal points. Negative space is what makes hardscape read as a *subject* rather than one texture among many.
3. **Front-to-back scale mismatch.** Large-scale material at the back collapses depth and flattens the scene.
4. **Mismatched material families.** Mixing rock types or wood tones breaks the "one place" illusion; each mismatched material reads as a competing element.

**Layout shapes:** *triangular* (rises from one corner to a peak, then slopes — the natural choice for a tall box, with the waterfall as the peak); *concave* (two raised side masses, open middle — good if the viewing zone is centred); *convex* (centre mass — the layout both traditions avoid for the *primary* mass, workable as a secondary background peak).
