## Vinegar is too weak, and flow is what decides the rate

**Wrong:**

```
Drop white vinegar on the rock. No fizz -> "it's inert."
```

Direct sourced challenge: "the 'vinegar test' doesn't work, period. If drops of vinegar are put on a rock that is calcium carbonate, the vinegar is too weak of acid to 'fizz' and bubble… You need to hit the rock with drops of a more concentrated acid such as hydrochloric acid."

**Right:**

```
1. Immerse each candidate rock in vinegar overnight, watch for slow bubbling.
2. Retest anything ambiguous with dilute HCl or a descaler.
3. Then the real test: soak the actual rocks in a bucket of RO/soft water
   for 2-4 weeks and measure KH. Only this catches slow leachers.
```

Oak extract also works as a hobby-friendly weak acid — it produces "clearly visible gas bubbles on the stone. This reaction does not occur with non-calcareous stones." Geological reference: limestone "bubbles vigorously" with dilute HCl while **dolomite reacts only feebly** — so a weak reaction is not a clean pass.

**Rock classification:**

| Rock | Carbonate? | Verdict for soft-water builds |
|---|---|---|
| Slate | No | Inert; flat slabs give egg-laying surfaces |
| **Dragon stone / Ohko** | **No — CORRECTION to common folklore** | Argillaceous (clay) rock, classified inert by three independent sources. Wash thoroughly, holes hold mud, brittle. Still acid-test your actual pieces — batch variation is real |
| Lava rock | No | Inert, very porous; small pieces may float |
| Quartz | No | Inert and non-permeable. The "high-silica rock causes diatoms" belief is debunked |
| **Seiryu / Ryuoh / "Mini Landscape"** | **YES — limestone** | Avoid. Also Frodo, Elephant Skin, Black Pagoda, Tsingy, Grey Rocks |
| River pebbles | Often YES | "Most of the time, they are limestone." Never assume a rounded stone is inert |

Blunt list of what IS calcium carbonate: seashells, coral, Seiryu, cuttlebone, aragonite, limestone, travertine, calcite, marble, tufa, coquina, chalk, holey rock.

**The measured data.** Spring water was acidified and titrated to a stable 6.6 pH / 6 dGH / 0 dKH, split across seven bottles with different rocks, and pH tracked over **120 days** with two calibrated meters. Result: "Limestone rock, coral, aragonite, crushed coral and crushed shells all buffered very slightly acid 6.6 pH water up to a **7.6 to 7.9 pH** quite well." The ceiling is 7.6-7.9, no higher.

**The rate law is the part that matters for a waterfall:** "the dissolution rate is closely related to the surface area and the water flows over that area. A bag of crushed coral inside a filter will raise the pH to 7.5 in a matter of days. A chunk of holey rock in relatively still water might take **months**." **A waterfall pours high-flow water over your hardscape continuously — that is the fast case, not the slow one.** This is the single strongest argument against any carbonate rock in a waterfall path.

**The soft-water invertebrate constraint:** dwarf shrimp from soft-water biotopes (*Caridina logemanni*, Taiwan Bee, *C. cantonensis*, *C. serrata*) "do not like the presence of carbonate hardness at all — the KH therefore shouldn't permanently be above **2 dKH**." And the trap in the usual workaround: active soil "doesn't have infinite absorption capacities. If it is constantly 'fed' with calcium carbonate, its buffering capacity will soon be exhausted," after which KH rises "slowly and hardly noticeably."

**Etching Seiryu in 30% HCl for 3-4 days slows the reaction; it does not stop it, and in a waterfall's flow the surface re-exposes.** Not recommended.
